How to cut agency/RPO spend by building sourcing in-house

If your internal recruiting team is busy and a role is still open, the usual move is to send it out. A contingency firm takes the search, or an RPO engagement absorbs a book of reqs. The hire gets made. The invoice arrives. Next quarter, you do it again.

That loop is expensive because it is designed to be: you pay for effort you do not keep. The way out is not a cheaper agency. It is to build the sourcing capability inside your team so you fill more roles in-house and stop paying the markup every time.

What you are actually paying for

A contingency recruiter does real work: market mapping, outreach, screening, coordination. The standard price, as we put it on the homepage, is a 20% contingency fee on first-year salary, due when the hire is made. You do not pay if they fail — and you also do not keep the pipeline or the relationships if they succeed. The next similar role starts the fee over.

RPO is the same idea at program scale: a retained recruiting function, often under your brand, for a book of hires. Useful during a surge. Still a vendor system. When the contract ends, the process and the candidate data typically go with the provider.

Neither model is a scandal. Both are rentals. If you already have an in-house TA team, the question is whether those invoices are a burst of capacity — or how you source every year.

The numbers, with the sources attached

RecruitCloud's pricing page uses three reference points for the spend this kind of system is meant to replace:

1. An agency placement in the $15,000–$35,000 per hire range. That band is consistent with a 20% contingency fee on mid-to-senior salaries — for example, 20% of a $100,000 role is $20,000; 20% of a $150,000 role is $30,000. It is a reference range, not a quote for your reqs. Run the math on your own average starting salary and fee.

2. RPO at roughly $200,000–$500,000 for 50 hires a year. That is the planning range we use on the pricing page. Contracts vary by model (per-hire, retainer, hybrid, project), so compare it to your invoices rather than treating it as a survey average.

3. A widely cited cost-per-hire figure of about $4,700. That number comes from SHRM. In April 2022, SHRM reported that the average cost per hire was nearly $4,700. The more recent public figure is higher: SHRM's 2025 benchmarking reports put average cost-per-hire at $5,475 for nonexecutive roles and $35,879 for executive roles (2,371 SHRM members, surveyed January 9–March 3, 2025; released October 15, 2025).

One caveat, because these numbers get misused: SHRM's cost-per-hire is a blended recruiting cost — it can include agency fees, job boards, recruiter pay, and systems, divided by hires. It is not "the cost of an in-house hire with zero vendor spend." The number that matters in a build decision is what *you* already pay vendors, plus what it costs to run sourcing on your own team.

What the gap looks like in practice

Keep the comparison to arithmetic on the fee you already know.

Say you fill 10 roles a year through agencies at a 20% fee, and those roles average $150,000 in first-year salary. That is 10 × $30,000, or $300,000 a year, before internal interview time. At a $100,000 average salary, 10 placements are $200,000; 20 placements are $400,000.

Put SHRM's 2025 nonexecutive average next to it, only as a benchmark: $5,475 × 10 hires is about $55,000. Even if your true in-house cost lands higher — hard-to-fill roles, or a fuller fully-loaded recruiter cost — the order of magnitude is the point. Contingency fees stack. A system you run at internal cost does not reset to 20% on every accepted offer.

RPO can look cheaper than agencies at volume. The pricing-page reference of ~$200,000–$500,000 for 50 hires is still a large, recurring program cost — and the capability still sits on their side of the contract.

Why the spend comes back every year

Vendor sourcing does not transfer. The outreach that got replies, the map of who would take a conversation, the rejected-but-worth-a-look candidates — that work is the vendor's book of business. You bought a hire, not a system.

That is why "we'll just use agencies less next year" rarely holds. The "one-off" requisition has a sibling six months later, and without an in-house pipeline you are back on the phone with the same firms. Buying another SaaS seat does not automatically fix it — plenty of teams still send overflow out because the tool never got adopted. The stall is on workflow, data quality, recruiter adoption, and governance.

What "building sourcing in-house" actually means

It does not mean hiring one more recruiter and hoping the agency line item shrinks. It means standing up a sourcing system your team operates: agents that continuously surface off-market, signal-matched candidates for your roles; sequencers that do the multi-channel follow-up; an intent-signal data layer (tenure, promotions, team changes) in a store you own; and a live pipeline on top of the ATS you already run.

The ATS stays. The recruiters stay. Sourcing and engagement are no longer rented: your team runs the system, and the output stays in your environment.

A practical path, not a rip-and-replace

You do not have to insource every req on day one. Executive search, a new geography, or a surge can still justify a specialist.

Map the spend. Pull 12 months of agency fees, RPO invoices, and overlapping tool costs, by role family. If you cannot name what you spent and what you got, you are not ready to brief a build. This is the first step of how we start.

Pick one role family. Choose a cluster you hire repeatedly, where past hires make the profile knowable, and where agency spend is concentrated. Stand up a live pipeline your recruiters can run.

Wire it into what you already own. Do not migrate ATSs as part of a sourcing project. Plug the new layer into the system of record so recruiters can see candidates, signals, and messages without a side process.

Keep overflow honest. Use agencies for true exceptions while the first system comes up — not for the same role family, out of habit, once the in-house pipeline is live.

Decide what happens if you part ways. If you lose the data and start over, you are still renting. If you keep the agents, sequences, data, dashboard, and playbooks, you are building an asset. That is the ownership contract.

What you keep when the invoices stop

An agency or RPO can reduce time-to-fill for a season. They cannot leave you the machine. When we build with a team, you keep all of it if we stop working together — no lock-in, and no platform that owns your pipeline. FAQ

How much does an agency hire actually cost?

It depends on the fee and the salary. A 20% contingency fee on a $100,000 role is $20,000; on a $150,000 role it is $30,000. That is why our pricing page uses $15,000–$35,000 per placement as a reference range. Use your own average salary and fee.

What is a reasonable cost-per-hire to compare against?

Use a source, and say which year it is from. SHRM's 2022 write-up cited an average of nearly $4,700. The 2025 benchmarking reports put the average at $5,475 for nonexecutive hires and $35,879 for executive hires. Those are blended recruiting costs, not a promise of what your first in-house search will cost.

Does bringing sourcing in-house mean we can never use an agency or RPO again?

No. It means agencies and RPO stop being the default for role families you hire regularly. Specialist search, a new market, or a short surge can still be a reason to go outside. The goal is to stop renting the core pipeline.

Will we have to replace our ATS to do this?

No. The ATS stays the system of record. The sourcing and engagement layer sits on top of it. Replacing your ATS is a different project, and not a prerequisite for cutting agency spend.

What happens to the system if we stop working with the team that built it?

You should keep it. In a RecruitCloud engagement, you keep the agents, sequences, data, dashboard, and playbooks. If a vendor cannot say that clearly, you are looking at a retainer, not a build.

How do we start without a year-long project?

Start with one role family and a scoped build. A Build Sprint stands up the first working system and trains your team to run it. Scope the timeline against your roles; do not take a date from a blog post. Book a build session and bring your current vendor spend.
