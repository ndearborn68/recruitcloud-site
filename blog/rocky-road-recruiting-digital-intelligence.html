The Rocky Road for Recruiting Firms and Digital Intelligence

Isaac Marks · November 24, 2026

If your best recruiter left on Friday, what would your firm still know how to do on Monday?

You’d have the ATS, the résumés, the client agreements, and a dashboard that says somebody sent 1,400 messages last month.

Would anybody know why your biggest client calls her before opening a search? Which candidates would move for the right job? Why the last three offers fell apart? Which job orders she quietly stopped working because the client was never going to hire?

I’ve built and sold a staffing firm. My read on most agency tech stacks is that they’re useful for getting through the day and worth very little on their own when it’s time to sell. The people learn from the work. The software records that it happened. Then the owner puts the firm on the market and a buyer asks how much of it can run without them.

Your father’s Oldsmobile had a big trunk, too

A hundred thousand résumés. Twenty recruiters. Forty signed client agreements. Sounds impressive in a sales deck. Then you open the trunk.

How many of those candidates are still interested? Which clients sent profitable, fillable work last year? How much of that came through relationships you personally handle? A signed agreement tells me someone agreed to your terms. Show me who keeps sending you business and why.

Among staffing agencies sold on BizBuySell from 2021 through 2025, the median price was 2.69 times seller’s discretionary earnings. The lower quartile was 2.04 times. I’d spend my time showing a buyer how the firm makes money when I’m out of the room. Where do the orders come from? Who knows which ones to take? What does the team do when a search gets stuck? What digital intelligence changes

On All-In, David Friedberg walks through three changes. Computers digitized work. The internet connected the information. Digital intelligence gives companies a reason to rethink how the work gets done. Later, Chamath Palihapitiya asks how much competitive know-how you expose when you run your workflows through somebody else’s product. Friedberg at 8:15 · Palihapitiya at 38:34

Here’s the agency version. Business development sees the hiring signal. The sourcer finds people. The sequencer knows who replied and what they said. The recruiter finds out what the hiring manager actually means by “senior.” Billing knows whether the placement made money. Connect those records and you can see which hiring signals turn into real job orders, where delivery stalls, and which clients are worth another call.

Google’s $10 million bid for de-identified Spirit Airlines business data puts a price on one kind of operating knowledge. A bankruptcy judge approved the deal at a November hearing, and Google said the data could help improve its products and AI models. A public webpage tells you where an airline flies. Its internal records can show what it took to get a plane off the ground. Spirit bankruptcy filing · Reuters on the court approval

Your agency generates its version of that knowledge every day. The job post says “regional operations manager.” Your recruiter learns the client will flex on title for somebody who’s opened three sites. She also learns that two good candidates walked after finding out “occasional travel” meant three weeks a month. When that job opens again, who knows what she learned?

I’ve used Pin and Juicebox. Here’s where I’d push back.

Juicebox wants to run the outreach. If you use its agent’s built-in email sequencing, the sends run through the mailboxes you connect there. Juicebox lists one mailbox on Starter, three per seat on Growth, and six per seat on Business. It supports multiple senders and offers ATS integrations and exports, so there’s a way to take a shortlist elsewhere. The dependency starts when sender setup, follow-up rules, and reply history all live inside the sourcing platform. How much of your outreach operation do you want to rebuild the next time you change how you source? Juicebox pricing · Juicebox sequences · Juicebox integrations and exports

Juicebox also brings interview scorecards, rejection reasons, and withdrawals from Ashby and Greenhouse back into sourcing. That feedback is valuable. Its public agreement allows it to use anonymized or aggregated data derived from customer interactions for product development, and it restricts customers from using Juicebox output or service-derived data to build or improve their own AI systems. Juicebox says it doesn’t use customer data for model training. I’d capture my team’s original feedback and decisions in the agency’s own records so we can use what we learned on the next search. Juicebox ATS Intelligence · Services Agreement, §§2.1.9 and 3.6 · Juicebox Trust Center

Pin gives you an export button. Read what happens when you leave. Pin tunes its ranking from recruiter feedback and stores what it learns about the job in its workspace. Its published policy requires you to stop using and delete Pin-supplied data when the subscription ends, with an exception for ATS or CRM records of candidates you contacted or engaged. Exporting candidates is useful. Your firm also needs the original reasons your team adjusted the search, where it found evidence of a fit, and what the client ended up accepting. Pin Acceptable Use Policy · Pin job memory documentation

Moon is coming for the placement fee. Moon Labs sells a managed recruiting service directly to employers. Its agents source, rank, and reach out. Its recruiters screen candidates and sell the role. The published fee is 5% to 25% of base salary, payable on hire. If your client sees your value as a shortlist and a few booked interviews, Moon already sells them that outcome. What does your firm know about their business and their talent market that earns the next call? Moon’s service and pricing

I call it a wallactomy when you pay to source, pay to engage the way the vendor packaged it, and still have to ask the recruiter what worked. The wallet has been removed and the original condition persists.

Source in one place. Sequence in another.

Use specialized tools. Let AI be the glue that connects them. Keep the recruiting history, the decisions, and the rules your team relies on in a system the agency controls.

I’d run engagement through a dedicated sequencer with multiple mailboxes set up around who owns the replies. Keep outreach separate from the domain you rely on for client conversations, offers, and invoices. You can use a different authenticated domain that the agency controls and clearly identify the recruiter and firm. Spam complaints can hurt sending reputation. Keep volume in check, watch bounces and complaints, and make sure opt-outs follow the person across tools. Gmail sender guidelines · Yahoo guidance on separating mail streams

Go back to that regional operations search. Before anything goes out, check the ATS and CRM for prior contact and opt-outs. The sequencer handles the send and stops follow-up when the candidate replies. The reply says they’d consider it at $160,000 if travel stays under one week a month. AI pulls those details into a note linked to the message. The recruiter checks it and picks up the conversation.

Six months later, when the client opens the same role, a different recruiter can see which employers produced good candidates, what people actually said about the travel, and what changed before somebody accepted. The agency can follow the trail back to the original hiring signal and forward to a paid placement. If you change the sequencer, that record still belongs to the team.

That’s the brain. It gives your recruiter back the afternoon they would have spent piecing together the last search across five tabs.

Build a firm you can leave for dinner

Start with one desk and its last 30 closed searches. Include the ones that died. Connect the hiring signal, the job order, the outreach, the outcome, and the gross profit. Ask the recruiters to fill in the reasons their tools missed. Give a repeat search to someone who’s never worked the desk and see where they get stuck.

Run that test before you renew another platform. Run it before you sell the firm. Run it on a Friday afternoon, too. If the team can figure out the next move while you’re having dinner with your family, you’ve built something worth scaling.
